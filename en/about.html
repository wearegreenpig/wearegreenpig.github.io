Subversive Imagination: Green Pig

A performance company made by people with the conviction that ideas change the world and with burning sensibility. We are people who came together to make theater that diagnoses a civilization proceeding without doubt, or without doubting. We are people who came together to make theater that thinks about progress in subject matter and artistic form. Green Pig's work is a manual, or a tool, for resistance or exodus.

Those Who Come, Those Who Go, Those Who Stay

By Park Sanghyeon · 2018

The summer of 2008 was particularly hot. We were rehearsing Grief Not Fully Mourned on the third floor of a building in Jeongneung. Even after changing into dry clothes, we would be soaked in sweat within five minutes. Yoon Hansol brought a virtually brand-new used air-conditioner from a friend. Jeong Jaesuk, a reporter from the JoongAng Ilbo, happened to swing by just then and introduced our space in the paper as “a rehearsal studio with a cool, air-conditioned breeze.” So, with that, Green Pig became known as the theater company with an above-ground rehearsal studio, the company that grills beef and drinks beer on the balcony, the company that runs the air-conditioning in summer and the heater in winter. A year before that, Kim Haksu used to go around with the two of us looking for a rehearsal space. He later founded a company called Sanineomeo, which has lately been eating away at his wallet. With Days Like a Painting, which we put on the year before, Yoon Hansol, Kim Haksu and I lost a total of 80 million won. Since then, I have called that show ‹Days Like Shit›.

A leftist from affluent Gangnam, Yoon Hansol moved north of the river to Gangbuk, and in 2009 climbed up to Namsan with Who Landed a Missile Punch on Muhammad Ali's Temple?, sniffing around the establishment. In 2011 he received the Yonkang Arts Award from the Doosan Art Center. In 2012 Brain Surgery won the Best Play Award at the Korea Drama Awards, and last year he even took the Kim Sang-yeol Theater Award, the kind of prize that only goes to people who win prizes. With Step-memory: Return of the Repressed he has been to Japan, to Germany, and so on.

Among the founding members, Lee Dongyeong remains. Hwang Miyeong joined for Grief Not Fully Mourned and played the alien. Jung Yanga followed Hwang into the company. Among Yoon Hansol's juniors from Hanyang University, Park Haneul is still with us. Jeon Sunghyun went to study at the K-Arts School of Drama and settled down there as a teaching assistant. Come to think of it, lots of people have left. We have had some new blood, but they are not fully ripe yet. Oh, then there is the alluringly middle-aged Choi Jiyeon.

And as time passed, some left this world for good. Yoon Yeongseon and Kim Donghyeon, who were family to us even before Green Pig was founded…

Hopefully in the next decade, this will become a home where many young people stay for a long time and shine on their own.

The Uneasy Theater of Green Pig

By Cho Mansu (theater critic; dramaturg of Jin and Jun, Psychopath, Chijeong (Passion) and I'm the King of Acting) · 2018

The adjective “green” does not sit naturally next to the noun “pig”. It is not that Green Pig means to represent some kind of vegan hog or pacifist swine. The visual image the combination creates is very uneasy: an angry green pig glares back at you. For over a decade since its founding in 2006, this angry green pig has made the Korean theater scene quite uncomfortable. It would not let audiences simply spend a moving evening at the theater. Green Pig opposes the comfortable union of thing and thing, of thing and sign. Such a peaceful union is a dominant discourse that hides something within. So ultimately, Green Pig refuses to let theater repeat the dominant discourse, defining itself as “a subversive imagination that dreams of overthrow” or “a manual for resistance or exodus.” Green Pig is a community that shares the will to overturn, in its imagination, a society that dreams of being green but rots in the bloated flesh of its desires (pig), along with that society's discourse. Its work is not to repeat the given discourse but to make a manual filled with other discourses it has newly made. This is not a noble act, nor a moral lesson. For Green Pig, it is a fun game.

However, this game is sometimes dangerous. It may have been a game for them, but the authorities may not have seen it that way. Or perhaps the authorities saw Green Pig as a mere toy in their own political game. During the blacklist scandal, Green Pig was among the very first to be excluded from state funding.

Green Pig is organized around Park Sanghyeon and Yoon Hansol. Park and Yoon share the philosophy of Green Pig but differ in how they practice it. This explains why, apart from a very few works such as Jin and Jun and Chijeong (Passion) (written by Park, directed by Yoon), the two usually pursue their own work independently.

Park Sanghyeon, both a director and a playwright, favors a structuralist approach to writing. He clashes ostensibly unrelated elements to create meaning, as in the plays he wrote: Assassins, Jin and Jun, Psychopath, Chijeong (Passion) and The Whistleblowers.

Assassins, Green Pig's first production, is a good illustration of Park's writing. The title refers to assassins in its Sino-Korean sense and to “terrorists” in English. On one hand it enacts the “Biographies of Assassins” from Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, mixing in anecdotes of Kim Gu, the leader of the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea. It also includes the stories of modern anarchist terrorists and of terrorists from Chechnya. These seemingly unrelated episodes from ancient China, modern Korea and contemporary Chechnya resonate with one another and form a meaning different from the one each has on its own. The negative word “terrorist” is examined from a completely different angle when it touches Kim Gu's struggle for independence. Is there, after all, any difference between terrorists and the patriots Yun Bong-gil and Lee Bong-chang, who threw bombs at the Japanese emperor, soldiers and politicians as part of the armed struggle for liberation from Japanese imperialism? Park by no means sympathizes with terror as indiscriminate violence against civilians, but he brings back the political motives of terror and tries to listen more closely to the voices of those who commit it.

The same is true of Chijeong (Passion). By presenting a play titled Chijeong (a crime of passion), Park hopes the audience will notice that read backwards the word becomes jeongchi, “politics”. The reversal is more than wordplay on similar sounds. He tries to reveal the “politics” contained in “chijeong”, a word for ethically unsound, socially dangerous love. Park is taking issue with the violence by which ruling power dismisses positions different from its own as “unethical”.

Unlike Park, who relates unrelated things to build a single whole, Yoon Hansol breaks apart what looks like a whole, deconstructing it and playing with it. Yoon also directs plays by writers such as Park Sanghyeon, Yoon Mihyun, Ko Jaegwi and Ahn Jaeseung, but many of the works he has directed are devised theater made collectively: Without Vinker I'm Too Lonely, Step-memory: Return of the Repressed, I'm the King of Sex, Commie: A Study for Rehabilitation (working title), The Way of Story, the Way of Song – Demo Version and I'm the King of Acting.

Yoon presents a concept and tries to strip from it everything that is not essential. For example, when he presents the word “sex,” he tries to show that sex is not about the object of sex but is desire itself. So for him, sex is a verb that contains its object. “To have sex with someone” is not the essence. “To have sex” is no different from “to desire,” and thus equivalent to verbs like “to read” or “to write.”

In The Way of Story, the Way of Song – Demo Version, Yoon does not accept the traditional genre of pansori as a finished, unchangeable form. For him, pansori is simply a way of singing a story. So he sets out to experience, as if for the first time, the way of singing a story. As a result he sings pansori in a completely different way, and makes a new way in which the story comes across even better. In I'm the King of Acting he asks about the essence of acting. Parodying TV audition shows, Green Pig's actors set acting that technically realizes the fiction of a role against acting that reveals the actor's own self.

Besides these two, Green Pig has young directors and actors. Jeon Sunghyun, who wrote 174517 and ‹Cheolsu Chronicles› and directed The Investigation (Oświęcim), also writes for the devised works. Park Hyunji, who directed Behind Cut, brings Green Pig fresh energy.

Devising with Green Pig

By Kim Minseung · 2017

# Art

The playwright David Mamet once said that when we go to the theater, “we all become friends here and find out what the heck is happening in this world.” He added that if that is not what we want, we are after cheap entertainment, not art.

Art. I have started off with too big a word, one I won't be able to live up to. I merely wanted to talk about why, if we are to go to the theater instead of watching TV, having a drink, seducing a stranger or going to pray, we should do so. This question has always made me uneasy, and (I think) it was one of the questions we could never avoid in the Green Pig productions I worked on. Why do we go to see plays? And why do we make them?

The question seems to become sharper in devised theater than in work where a text comes first. If there is a play you want to stage, that alone can be a reason to make theater. But devising doesn't even have that. Without that “something” for all of us to hold on to, we have to set off precariously from the very simple idea of “let's make a show together.”

# Bats

I was the dramaturg for I'm the King of Sex, Anyway, Baek Seok, Tree Won't Seek for Shoe Store, Brain Surgery, Commie: A Study for Rehabilitation (working title) and The Way of Story, the Way of Song – Demo Version, and did the writing for Unwanted, Na Hye-seok and 1984. I am not sure myself what the difference between the two is. In a devising system it is hard to tell the dramaturg's role from the writer's. All I did was dig through material, analyze other people's writing, sometimes add to it, and sometimes write, briefly or at more length. And I spent a lot of time arguing for no reason, butting in, insisting even when I knew I was wrong, and losing my temper when others did the same. In a column I once wrote about our work1, I compared the dramaturg's role in devised theater to a bat. Everyone knows how poor a bat's eyesight is. Since it can barely see, a bat sends out ultrasound to keep checking its own position and that of things around it as it flies desperately through the dark cave. That felt very much like what a dramaturg has to do in devising. But did I ever send out my ultrasound at the right moment, like a bat? I have never once felt that I contributed well as a dramaturg, or felt proud watching the finished show. What is true is that I threw a lot of uncomfortable noise at the people I worked with.

# Map

With work where the text comes first, there is at least some kind of map, accurate or not. Devising, by contrast, is closer to a journey that starts without a map and finds its way while drawing one. There were even times when the map was finished only when the show was. Finding the way while drawing the map is arduous, very inefficient and likely to fail, but the process is quite interesting. In Commie: A Study for Rehabilitation (working title) there was another process that never made it into the show. We tried to build a scene out of all the dialogue in the scene where K is arrested in Kafka's The Trial. We all put a lot of work into it, but in the end it never reached the stage. For 1984, I wrote several versions of the script for the final scene, but none of them worked, and in the end, instead of writing a script, we rewrote the last chapter of the novel 1984 itself. Such wandering may have been inevitable for us at the time, and it has its own meaning. But how such a process is carried into the finished show is another matter, and that is homework Green Pig's devising still has to do. In a somewhat different context, didn't Michel Houellebecq say it in one of his works? “The map is more interesting than the territory.”

# Theater

When I once wrote about Green Pig's work (in the same column mentioned above), I compared theater to revolution. I no longer agree with that. Of course, I have no alternative.

What does it mean to watch a play? What do we hope to gain by rushing to the theater and crouching in a dark, cramped space, staring endlessly at a stage, instead of drinking, having sex or joining a protest?

And what does it mean to make a play? Why did we choose to take the risk of devising? And were we truly at risk in devising?

These are heavy questions, then as now.

1 “Utterances of a Dramaturg” in Writing to the Stage (National Theater Company of Korea, 2013). This essay was written as a 2017 rewriting of that column.
